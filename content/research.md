## Research

Our research addresses a fundamental question in evolutionary biology: how do speciation mechanisms operate across the tree of life? We approach this through two complementary lines of work: (i) integrating population genomics, historical biogeography, and molecular ecology to investigate how geoclimatic processes drive population divergence and shape vertebrate diversity, and (ii) developing computational tools for demographic inference, combining coalescent simulation with machine learning.

---

### Diversification of the South American Dry Diagonal

The Dry Diagonal, encompassing the Caatinga, Cerrado, and Chaco biomes, is the largest arid and semi-arid region in the Neotropics. Our lab has produced a series of genomic studies testing the roles of rivers, climate fluctuations, and gene flow in shaping vertebrate diversity across this region. We demonstrated that the Sao Francisco River acts as a biogeographic barrier for multiple vertebrate lineages (Coelho, Guillory & Gehara 2022), uncovered cryptic diversity and reticulate evolution in whiptail lizards and broad-snouted caimans (Magalhaes et al. 2025; Ornellas, Coelho et al. 2025), and revealed landscape genetic processes driving gene flow in highland frog populations (de Andrade Lima et al. 2025). This work culminated in an invited review in *Molecular Ecology* (Guillory et al. 2024), the first comprehensive treatment of geoclimatic drivers of diversification across the entire Dry Diagonal.

### North American Vertebrates

Collaborations on North American snakes and birds have addressed speciation in the context of hybrid zones, reticulate evolution, and extinction dynamics. Work with Frank Burbrink's group resolved long-standing taxonomic questions in ratsnakes and milksnakes through genomic analyses of hybridization (*Systematic Biology* 2021; *Evolution* 2021), while collaboration with Brian Smith used demographic modeling to reconstruct the history of extinction in eastern North American birds (*Proceedings B* 2021). A study with Gregory Thom demonstrated that tropical valleys are deeper barriers for montane birds than previously appreciated (*Nature Communications* 2021).

### Computational Methods for Demographic Inference

We develop statistical and machine learning tools for inferring population history from genomic data. The hABC method (Gehara et al. 2017, *Molecular Ecology*) and PipeMaster software have been adopted by at least 32 independent research groups worldwide, appearing in approximately 44 studies spanning reptiles, amphibians, birds, mammals, marine turtles, insects, crabs, and plants in journals including *Nature Communications*, *Molecular Ecology*, *Proceedings B*, *Systematic Biology*, and *Journal of Biogeography*. We are currently developing [PipeMaster](https://github.com/gehara/PipeMaster) 2.0, featuring a native C coalescent engine, neural network-based parameter estimation, and a graphical user interface for whole-genome data.

### Future Directions

Our empirical and computational research lines are converging. We have generated a chromosome-level reference genome for *Ameivula ocellifera* (~1.64 Gb, scaffold N50 > 100 Mb, 99.6% BUSCO completeness), a whiptail lizard distributed across the Dry Diagonal lowlands. Combined with whole-genome resequencing from natural hybrid zones along elevational gradients in the Chapada Diamantina, this system allows us to study how landscape-mediated selection shapes hybridization and genomic adaptation. Preliminary results reveal asymmetric introgression, with lowland alleles selectively prevented from spreading into highland populations at loci involved in hypoxia response and metabolic regulation.

We are also developing AI-based approaches for species delimitation and phylogeography, a new direction that integrates deep learning with population genetic theory.
